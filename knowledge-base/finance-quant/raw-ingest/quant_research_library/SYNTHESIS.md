# SYNTHESIS

The central intellectual line through this library is the move from observable market traces to latent decision logic. The raw traces are holdings, trades, orders, quotes, prices, news, narratives, and agent communications. The latent objects are investor demand curves, reward functions, beliefs, constraints, risk budgets, execution objectives, and regime-dependent heuristics. A serious trading system cannot stop at return prediction. It has to ask: whose behavior created the signal, what constraints made that behavior repeatable, how much capital can exploit it, and how will execution change the signal after deployment?

The first lineage is demand-system asset pricing. Koijen and Yogo's demand-system framework is the cleanest academic route from holdings to investor decision logic. Instead of treating prices as mysterious equilibrium outputs, it estimates investor demand over assets and uses market clearing to infer elasticities, substitution, and pricing effects. Gabaix, Koijen, Richmond, and Yogo's Asset Embeddings extends that logic into learned representations: assets and investors can be placed in latent spaces based on observed portfolio behavior. This is directly relevant to reverse-engineering trader or fund logic. It suggests that a system should not merely ask whether a stock has alpha; it should ask which investors are likely to demand it, what they substitute away from, and which flows actually matter for valuation.

The inelastic-markets hypothesis explains why this matters economically. If demand curves are not infinitely elastic, flows can move prices far more than frictionless textbook models imply. That makes investor identification valuable. A small flow from the wrong investor may matter less than a larger flow from a price-insensitive buyer, but a concentrated demand shock from a constrained or benchmarked institution can create persistent pressure. The hedge-fund and 13F literature connects here: Best Ideas, 13F alpha papers, hidden-holdings research, and misreporting studies all ask whether public portfolio traces reveal skill, conviction, or deception. The warning is obvious: reported holdings are useful but incomplete and strategically contaminated.

The second lineage is empirical asset pricing with machine learning. Gu, Kelly, and Xiu, Kelly-Pruitt-Su, Chen-Pelger-Zhu, Nagel, and the newer AI Asset Pricing Models paper provide the benchmark for serious ML return prediction and stochastic discount factor estimation. This literature is the adult version of AI alpha discovery. It cares about cross-sectional structure, factor exposures, pricing errors, characteristic interactions, and validation. For a founder building a trading system, this matters because most LLM or retail-AI strategy demos are not competing against nothing. They are competing against a mature empirical asset-pricing literature with strict baselines.

The third lineage is statistical hygiene. Cont's stylized facts, Bailey and Lopez de Prado's backtest-overfitting work, the Deflated Sharpe Ratio, Harvey-Liu-Zhu on factor multiple testing, and Lo's Sharpe-ratio inference all exist because markets are hostile to naive validation. Returns are fat-tailed, non-normal, regime-dependent, serially distorted, and vulnerable to data mining. Any system that generates many candidate strategies - especially with LLMs - needs this layer first. Otherwise it will optimize until it finds a false positive. This is the major failure mode of vibe-coded quant systems: they automate hypothesis generation but do not automate hypothesis destruction.

The fourth lineage is market microstructure and execution. Almgren-Chriss gives the optimal-execution base: trading has impact, risk, and implementation shortfall. Cont-Kukanov-Stoikov quantifies order-book event impact and order-flow imbalance. Avellaneda-Stoikov formalizes market making with inventory constraints. Easley, Lopez de Prado, O'Hara, and Zhang update microstructure thinking for the machine age. These papers convert a backtest from a signal toy into a trading problem. Many strategies fail not because the signal is imaginary, but because the edge is smaller than spread, fees, slippage, market impact, latency disadvantage, or adverse selection.

The fifth lineage is inverse reinforcement learning and imitation learning. Ng-Russell, Abbeel-Ng, Ziebart, Ho-Ermon, AIRL, and DAgger form the CS foundation. Finance-specific work by Halperin, Dixon, Kolm/Ritter, and Roa-Vicens applies the idea to investment objectives and limit-order books. This literature is central to the user's target: reverse-engineering trader decision logic. IRL asks a sharper question than supervised prediction: what reward function or objective would make this observed behavior rational? In finance, that question is hard because demonstrations are partial, the state is hidden, other agents react, and expert behavior may be constrained by mandates, taxes, liquidity, career risk, or reporting rules. The open opportunity is not generic IRL; it is constrained, uncertainty-aware IRL over financial traces with realistic costs and partial observability.

The sixth lineage is behavioral finance and psychometric inference. Barber-Odean, Odean, Barberis, Shiller, Tetlock, and transaction-based psychometric work show that investors are not simple utility maximizers. They overtrade, hold losers, chase narratives, react to media tone, overweight salient information, and express stable behavioral biases in financial records. This supports the idea that decision logic can be inferred from behavior, but it also warns against overclaiming. A trader's action may reflect bias, tax management, liquidity need, mandate constraint, or random noise. Good inference must output uncertainty, not personality fan fiction.

The seventh lineage is LLM trading agents and multi-agent orchestration. TradingAgents, TradingGPT, FinMem, GPT-Signal, QuantAgent, Trading-R1, TraderTalk, LiveTradeBench, and LLM experimental-market papers show a fast-growing ecosystem. The strongest use case is not autonomous live trading. It is research acceleration: summarizing filings, generating features, building hypothesis queues, comparing narratives, extracting event variables, and coordinating specialized agents under strict validation. Behavioral-consistency papers matter because an LLM agent that changes its trading policy after wording changes is not a robust strategy. LLM agents should sit upstream of deterministic research and risk systems, not bypass them.

The eighth lineage is pattern recognition and technical analysis under academic discipline. Lo-Mamaysky-Wang, Brock-Lakonishok-LeBaron, Park-Irwin, Sullivan-Timmermann-White, and micro-trading pattern-recognition papers separate testable pattern features from retail chart mythology. ICT and fair-value-gap language has weak academic support as standalone evidence. The academically defensible route is to translate any pattern claim into measurable variables - order-flow imbalance, spread behavior, realized volatility, liquidity gaps, volume clusters, event sequencing - then apply multiple-testing and execution controls.

## Load-bearing papers

1. Koijen & Yogo (2019), *A Demand System Approach to Asset Pricing* - the strongest holdings-to-demand framework.
2. Gabaix, Koijen, Richmond & Yogo (2025), *Asset Embeddings* - the clearest bridge from asset/investor traces to learned latent decision representations.
3. Gu, Kelly & Xiu (2020), *Empirical Asset Pricing via Machine Learning* - the ML asset-pricing benchmark every AI-alpha claim must face.
4. Cont, Kukanov & Stoikov (2014), *The Price Impact of Order Book Events* - the practical microstructure anchor for order-flow and execution-aware features.
5. Bailey et al. / Bailey & Lopez de Prado on backtest overfitting and Deflated Sharpe - the anti-fantasy layer for strategy generation.
6. Ziebart (2008) and Ho & Ermon (2016) - the core reward-inference and imitation-learning foundation.
7. Lo, Mamaysky & Wang (2000), *Foundations of Technical Analysis* - the legitimate academic version of pattern recognition in markets.

## Open research questions

First, can demand-system asset pricing and asset embeddings be turned into practical, near-real-time decision-logic inference from public holdings, flows, and filings? The literature is strong but often slower-moving than tradable systems need.

Second, can IRL work under financial partial observability? Real traders do not reveal their complete state, constraints, beliefs, or execution motives. Finance-specific IRL needs posterior uncertainty and causal discipline, not just reward recovery.

Third, can LLM agents be made behaviorally stable enough for research workflows? Current systems are useful for synthesis and feature ideation, but they are fragile as autonomous decision makers.

Fourth, how should strategy-generation systems price multiple testing in real time? LLMs can create infinite candidate signals. The validation layer must become as automated as the generation layer.

Fifth, can pattern-recognition models connect microstructure features to macro regimes? The thin area is not chart recognition; it is mapping micro order-flow patterns into larger economic states without overfitting.

## Open-access coverage

The strongest open-access coverage is in arXiv-heavy areas: IRL foundations, LLM trading agents, model extraction, some RL trading, and recent pattern-recognition/agent-based market simulations. NBER provides strong working-paper access for demand-system and AI asset-pricing anchors, though some final journal versions remain paywalled.

The most paywalled areas are classic finance journals, options foundations, market microstructure books, hedge-fund factor literature, and behavioral-finance journal articles. These should be requested through institutional access or interlibrary loan. The working-paper versions are often enough for building systems, but final journal versions should be checked before formal citation.

The practical conclusion is blunt: the highest-value build is not an LLM trader. It is a research-control system that combines investor-demand inference, strict statistical validation, execution-aware simulation, and LLM-assisted feature generation under hard guardrails. The core product advantage is not finding more ideas. It is killing bad ideas faster and preserving the few that survive demand logic, microstructure, and out-of-sample reality.
